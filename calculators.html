Read this before you trust the number

This is an estimate from a population formula and it can be off by 15% for you personally.1 Treat it as week one's experiment. After one four week cycle of eating the number and tracking the scale, you will know your real maintenance, which is worth more than any calculator.

Targets below roughly 1,500 kcal for men or 1,200 for women are hard to hit while getting adequate protein and micronutrients. If the calculator lands you there, prefer a smaller deficit over a longer period, add walking to widen the gap rather than cutting food further, and consider speaking to a dietitian.

How the maths works

Resting energy, Mifflin-St Jeor

The most accurate of the common prediction equations for the general population.1

(10 x kg) + (6.25 x cm) - (5 x age) + 5 for men
(10 x kg) + (6.25 x cm) - (5 x age) - 161 for women

This is what you would burn lying still all day. Nobody eats at this number.

Total daily energy expenditure

Resting energy multiplied by an activity factor between 1.2 and 1.9. This multiplier is the weakest link in the whole calculation, because it bundles your job, your steps, your fidgeting and your training into one guess. When in doubt, pick the lower option. Going to the gym four times a week is a 1.375 life rather than a 1.725 life.

The target

A percentage of expenditure rather than a flat number, so the deficit scales with your size. A 500 kcal cut is gentle for a 110 kg person and brutal for a 50 kg one.

The macros

Protein
Set first from bodyweight: 2.2 g/kg cutting, 2.0 losing slowly, 1.8 maintaining or gaining3
Fat
A floor, the greater of 0.7 g/kg or 22% of calories
Carbs
Whatever calories are left. This is the flexible one and the fuel for hard training

Rate of change

Using roughly 7,700 kcal per kg of bodyweight. It is a simplification, since early water shifts and later metabolic adaptation both distort it, but it sets a realistic expectation. Any plan promising faster than 1% of bodyweight a week is promising muscle loss.

One rep max

The average of two standard formulas, which diverge above roughly 10 reps:

Epley: weight x (1 + reps / 30)
Brzycki: weight x 36 / (37 - reps)

Both are estimates fitted to group averages. Yours can differ by 5% or more in either direction depending on the lift and your rep endurance.

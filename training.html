The training pyramid

The gym version of the same problem. People debate splits and rest timers while training twice a month with sets they could have doubled. Fix the base first.

Short answer

Train 2 to 4 times a week for years, take your sets to within 1 to 3 reps of failure, and beat your logbook over time. Those three carry about 75% of the result.

Then run 10 to 20 hard sets per muscle per week, hit each muscle twice, and keep the same handful of exercises long enough to progress them. Rest periods, tempo and advanced techniques come last and are worth a few percent.

Tier 1

Effort and progression

Short answer

Train 2 to 4 days a week. Take working sets to 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Use double progression: pick a rep range, add weight only once you hit the top of the range on every set. Log every set so you know what to beat.

Show up, for years

Three sessions a week is the honest sweet spot for someone with a full-time job. Two well-run full-body sessions still work. Six days is not a moral achievement. It is a schedule that breaks the first time you travel.

Attach training to something fixed. Same days, same time, bag packed the night before, gym on the commute rather than across town. You are removing friction, not summoning motivation.

The 45 minute rule

A session you can finish in 45 minutes is one you will do on a bad day. Two hard compounds plus two or three accessories fits comfortably. Long sessions are mostly rest, phone, and exercises added out of anxiety.

Train close to failure

Take working sets to within 1 to 3 reps of failure. If you could have done five more, that set did very little. Meta-analysis suggests hypertrophy improves as sets get closer to failure, with the effect flattening in the last couple of reps.14

Reps in reserveHow it feelsUse it for
0, failureThe next rep will not moveOccasionally, on machines and isolations. Not on heavy barbell lifts.

Reps in reserve is a practical way to rate effort without testing your max every session.16

Rep range matters less than you think. Anywhere from 5 to 30 reps builds similar muscle provided the set is taken close to failure.13 Six to twelve is simply the most practical window: heavy enough to build strength, light enough that form holds and your joints stay comfortable.

Progressive overload

Muscle adapts to a demand that keeps rising. If the demand is flat for six months, so are you. Overload means any of:

  • More weight at the same reps
  • More reps at the same weight
  • An extra set
  • Better range of motion or control at the same numbers

Write it down. Notes app, paper, any free app. You cannot beat last week if you cannot remember last week, and you will not remember last week. This is the cheapest, highest return habit on the entire site. There is a free printable log if you prefer paper.

Double progression, the rule to use

Pick a range, say 8 to 12. Add weight only when you hit the top of the range on every set.

Progress slows as you get more trained. That is normal rather than failure. A beginner adds weight weekly. After two years it might be monthly on a given lift. Both are working.

Tier 2

Volume, frequency and exercise selection

Short answer

Run 10 to 20 hard sets per muscle per week, starting at the low end. Split them across two sessions per muscle. Choose one or two exercises per muscle group and keep them for months so you can actually progress them.

Volume

A hard set means a working set taken within a few reps of failure. Warm-ups do not count. Ten sets a week grows muscle in most people, and more sets tend to produce more growth up to a point.11 Twenty is near the top of what is usually useful. Start at the bottom and add only when progress stalls. You cannot add volume forever, so do not spend the budget in month one.

Compounds pay into several muscles at once. Three sets of rows credit your back and, partly, your biceps, so you do not need six extra curl sets to cover arms.

Work out your own weekly set target.

Frequency

At matched weekly volume, twice a week beats once by a small margin, and it makes sessions more manageable.12 Ten sets of back in one go is a slog where two lots of five is not. Three times a week is fine too, and the returns flatten out. This is the main reason upper/lower splits work so well across four days.

Exercise selection

One or two exercises per muscle group, held for months. You cannot apply progressive overload to a lift you swap out every session, and muscle confusion is a marketing phrase rather than a mechanism. Rotate exercises every few months if a lift stops feeling productive or a joint complains, not because you got bored in week two.

Choose by these four criteria

  1. Trains the muscle through a long range under load

    Full range beats a short heavy partial.

  2. Loadable and progressable

    You must be able to add weight in small increments. This is why machines and dumbbells eventually beat most bodyweight work.

  3. Comfortable and repeatable for you

    The best exercise that hurts your shoulder is not the best exercise. Anatomy varies, so substitute freely.

  4. Fails safely

    Missing a rep should be a non-event. Machines, dumbbells and cables qualify. Loaded barbells across your back need safeties or a spotter.

Tier 3

Rest, tempo and periodisation

Short answer

Rest 2 to 3 minutes on compounds and 1 to 2 on isolations. Control the lowering phase for about two seconds. Put the hardest lift first. When progress stalls for two or three weeks, take an easy week at half the sets. That covers almost everything in this tier.

Rest periods

Longer rest means more reps at a given weight, which means more volume. That is the entire mechanism, and it is why the old 60 second advice was counterproductive.15 If you are short on time, superset unrelated muscles such as calves with chest rather than cutting rest on your main lifts.

Tempo

Control the lowering phase, roughly two seconds, without counting. Do not bounce out of the bottom. Lift the weight with intent. Elaborate tempo prescriptions mainly reduce the load you can handle, and the returns do not justify the bookkeeping.

Exercise order

Hardest, most technical, most important lift first while you are fresh. That is usually a heavy compound. Isolations and machines at the end where fatigue costs you least.

Periodisation and deloads

For a recreational lifter, periodisation mostly means this: when progress stalls for two or three weeks across several lifts, take an easy week. Half the sets, or the same sets at around 60% of the weight. You usually come back stronger. That captures most of the benefit of any formal model without the spreadsheet.

Advanced techniques

Drop sets, rest-pause, myo-reps, partials and cluster sets are genuinely useful for one thing: getting an equivalent stimulus in less time. They do not out-grow straight sets taken near failure, and they generate more fatigue per unit of work. Use them when the clock is the constraint rather than as an upgrade.

Everything else people worry about

  • Free weights and machines both build muscle. Machines are safer to push hard, barbells load heavier. Use both.
  • Mind-muscle connection is mildly helpful on isolations and irrelevant on heavy compounds.
  • Training to failure on every set adds fatigue without adding growth. Save it for the last set of an isolation.
  • Long static stretching before lifting slightly reduces strength. Warm up with movement and lighter sets of the lift instead.
Foundation

Cardio and steps

Short answer

Walk 7,000 to 10,000 steps a day and do 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, which is the public health floor.17 Cardio does not kill your gains. Excessive cardio combined with insufficient food does.

Lifting shapes your body. Cardio keeps you alive longer. Cardiorespiratory fitness is one of the strongest predictors of all-cause mortality there is, so it is not optional just because you train for looks.19

Walking7,000 to 10,000 steps dailyBiggest lever on daily energy expenditure, near-zero recovery cost, fits a desk job. Mortality benefits rise up to roughly 8,000 to 10,000 steps.18
Easy cardio2 to 3 x 30 to 45 minBuilds the aerobic base at a conversational pace. Cycle, incline walk, row or swim.

Keep hard running away from leg sessions and you will never notice a conflict between the two.

Common questions

Do I need to lift heavy to build muscle?

No. Loads from roughly 30% to 80% of your one rep max build similar muscle when sets are taken close to failure. Heavy loads matter specifically for building maximal strength. Six to twelve reps is the practical default because it balances both.

Is soreness a sign of a good workout?

No. Soreness mostly tracks novelty, such as a new exercise, a long layoff, or lots of work in stretched positions. Experienced lifters grow while rarely getting sore. Judge progress by the logbook rather than the ache.

Which training split is best?

The one matching your available days that hits each muscle about twice a week. Full body for 2 to 3 days, upper/lower for 4, push/pull/legs for 5 to 6. The split is a scheduling tool rather than a training method.

Can I build muscle at home with bodyweight only?

Early on, yes. Push-ups, chin-ups, split squats and hinges taken near failure work well. The limitation is progression, since you eventually run out of ways to make a push-up harder. Bands, a pull-up bar and a pair of adjustable dumbbells solve most of it.

How long before I see results?

Strength improves within two to four weeks, largely through better technique and neural adaptation. Visible muscle change usually takes eight to twelve weeks. Fat loss shows on the scale within two weeks and in the mirror around six to eight. A realistic first year for a male beginner is 8 to 10 kg of muscle at best, and roughly half that for a female beginner, with both figures falling sharply in later years.

Do I need a belt, straps or a spotter?

Straps are genuinely useful, because they stop your grip capping your back training. A belt helps on heavy squats and deadlifts above roughly 80% of your max. Sleeves are comfort. None of it is required to start. If you bench alone, use a rack with safety pins rather than relying on a spotter who is not there.

What if I miss a week?

Nothing meaningful happens. You hold muscle for weeks without training, and strength returns within a session or two. Come back at about 90% of your previous weights for one week, then carry on. A missed week is a rounding error. Quitting because of a missed week is not.

Should I train through pain?

Muscular burn and general fatigue are fine. Sharp pain, joint-located pain, one-sided pain, or anything that lingers the next day means stop that exercise and substitute a pain-free alternative. Persistent pain is a physiotherapist's job rather than a website's.

Pick your split

Choose by the days you can protect on a bad week, not the days you would like to train. A three day plan you finish for a year beats a five day plan you abandon in March.

Short answer

Two or three days, run full body. Four days, run upper/lower. Five days, run upper/lower plus push/pull/legs. Every plan below covers the six movement patterns, trains each muscle about twice a week, and lands in the 10 to 16 weekly sets per muscle range.

How to run any of these

Warm up with five minutes of easy movement plus two or three lighter sets of your first exercise. Take working sets to 1 to 3 reps from failure. Rest 2 to 3 minutes on the first two exercises and 1 to 2 minutes after that. Log every set. When you hit the top of the rep range on all sets, add the smallest available weight next session. Run the plan for 8 to 12 weeks before changing anything.

The default

Three full body sessions of about 55 minutes, rotating A, B, C. If you have a full-time job and want one recommendation from this whole site, this is it. Each muscle gets trained three times a week at moderate per-session volume, which is easy to recover from and easy to progress.

4 days a week

Upper and lower, best return per hour

Two upper days and two lower days of about 55 minutes each. Everything gets trained twice a week with enough volume to grow and enough rest to recover. If you can genuinely protect four days, this is the most productive structure available.

5 days a week

Upper, lower, push, pull, legs

About 50 minutes each across five days. Only pick this if sleep and food are already solid, because five mediocre sessions are worse than four good ones. The advantage is shorter sessions rather than more growth.

Chest, back and shoulders land twice a week through upper plus push or pull. Legs land twice through lower plus legs. That is the whole design.

The parts people get wrong

Warming up

Five minutes of easy cardio to raise your temperature, then two or three ramping sets of your first exercise at roughly 50%, 70% and 85% of your working weight. That is it. Skip long static stretching before lifting, since it slightly reduces strength and does not prevent injury. Later exercises need one light set at most.

When you miss a day

Shift the week rather than doubling up. Missed Wednesday, train Thursday. Missed the whole week, resume where you left off at about 90% of your weights for one session. Never stack two hard sessions to catch up, because that is how a missed day becomes a missed month.

When to change the plan

After 8 to 12 weeks, or when progress on most lifts has stalled for two or three weeks despite eating and sleeping properly. Try an easy week first at half the sets, since most plateaus are accumulated fatigue. If it is a genuine stall, change exercises before changing the structure.

Substituting exercises

Swap freely within the same movement pattern. No squat rack, use leg press or split squats. Shoulder dislikes barbell bench, use dumbbells or a machine press. Nothing on these lists is sacred. The pattern checklist is what matters.

Deloads

Every 8 to 12 weeks, or whenever you feel systemically beaten up, take one week at half the sets with the same weights. You almost always come back stronger. Skipping deloads is how people end up taking three unplanned months off.

New to lifting?

Spend your first two or three weeks deliberately below your limit, around three sets of 8 to 10 at a weight you could do 15 times, learning the movements. Connective tissue adapts more slowly than muscle, and the soreness from going all out in week one is what makes people quit in week two. Read the cues first.

Exercise library

Form content is where the noise gets worst. Every lift has a hundred cues online and you cannot think about a hundred things under a loaded bar. Below is what actually changes the movement, plus the mistakes behind most gym injuries.

Three rules that apply to every lift here

1. Full range of motion under control. Half reps with more weight are worse, not braver. 2. Lower for about two seconds, lift with intent. No bouncing. 3. Sharp or joint pain means stop and substitute. Muscular burn is fine. A pinch in a shoulder is information.

Squat pattern

Quads and glutes. Pick one as your main knee bend and progress it for months.

Barbell back squat Quads, glutes

Cues

  1. Brace before you descend. Big breath into the belly, tighten as if bracing for a punch, hold it through the rep.
  2. Sit between your feet, not behind them. Knees travel forward over the toes, which is normal and safe.
  3. Drive the whole foot through the floor. Chest and hips rise together.

Common mistakes

  • Knees caving in under load. Think about spreading the floor with your feet.
  • Rounding the lower back at the bottom. Go only as deep as you can hold position.
  • Squatting in a rack with no safety pins set.
Stance
Shoulder width or slightly wider, toes out 15 to 30 degrees. Adjust until it feels strong, because hip anatomy varies and there is no universal stance.
Substitutes
Hack squat, leg press, goblet squat, Smith machine squat
Leg press Quads, glutes

Cues

  1. Feet mid-platform, whole foot in contact. Higher feet shifts work to glutes and hamstrings, lower to quads.
  2. Lower until your thighs approach your torso and stop before your hips roll off the pad.
  3. Stop just short of locking the knees to keep tension on the muscle rather than the joint.

Common mistakes

  • Letting the lower back round off the pad at the bottom. That is the injury position.
  • Quarter reps with a huge stack.
  • Pushing the knees with your hands.

The safest way to train quads hard. Failing a rep costs you nothing, which means you can genuinely push close to failure.

Bulgarian split squat Quads, glutes, balance

Cues

  1. Rear foot on a bench, front foot far enough forward that your front shin stays near vertical at the bottom.
  2. Lean the torso forward slightly for more glute, stay upright for more quad. Pick one and stay consistent.
  3. Drive through the front heel. The back leg is a kickstand, not a second engine.

Common mistakes

  • Standing too close to the bench, which jams the front knee.
  • Pushing off the back foot.
  • Starting with heavy dumbbells before you can balance. Bodyweight first.

Excellent value when equipment is limited. A pair of 20 kg dumbbells goes a long way here.

Hinge pattern

Hamstrings, glutes and the whole posterior chain. The most under-trained pattern and the one that most protects your back.

Romanian deadlift Hamstrings, glutes

Cues

  1. Push your hips back, do not bend down. The bar stays against your legs the whole way.
  2. Keep a slight knee bend and hold it. Knees do not travel. Hips do.
  3. Stop when your hamstrings reach full stretch, usually just below the knee, rather than when the bar reaches the floor.

Common mistakes

  • Rounding the lower back to chase depth. Depth comes from hamstring length, not spinal flexion.
  • Turning it into a squat by bending the knees.
  • Letting the bar drift away from the body, which multiplies the load on your back.

The best hamstring builder there is, and the lift that teaches you how to pick things up for the rest of your life. Use straps once grip becomes the limit.

Conventional or trap-bar deadlift Posterior chain

Cues

  1. Bar over mid-foot, shins close. Take the slack out of the bar before you pull. You should feel tension in the back first.
  2. Chest up, back flat, then push the floor away. Hips and shoulders rise together, so the hips should not shoot up first.
  3. Finish standing tall. No leaning back, no shrugging at the top.

Common mistakes

  • Jerking the bar off the floor from a loose position.
  • Rounding under heavy weight. Drop the load and rebuild the position.
  • Sets of 10 or more where form degrades rep after rep. Keep deadlifts to 3 to 6 reps.
Trap-bar is the better default

More upright torso, less lower-back demand, easier to learn, near-identical training effect. Deadlifts are also the most fatiguing lift on this page, so three hard sets is plenty.

Hip thrust Glutes

Cues

  1. Upper back on the bench, chin tucked slightly. Shins roughly vertical at the top.
  2. Drive through the heels and squeeze the glutes hard for a beat at the top.
  3. Ribs down. The movement is hip extension, not lower-back arching.

Common mistakes

  • Over-extending the lower back to fake height at the top.
  • Feet too far forward, turning it into a hamstring exercise.
  • Skipping a pad and then blaming the exercise.
Leg curl, seated or lying Hamstrings

Cues

  1. Control the return. Most of the value is in resisting the way back.
  2. Keep your hips pinned to the pad. No lifting off to gain leverage.
  3. Prefer the seated version, which trains the stretched position better.

Romanian deadlifts train hamstrings at the hip. Curls train them at the knee. You want both, and a couple of sets is enough.

Push pattern

Chest, shoulders and triceps, horizontal and vertical. You want both directions.

Bench press Chest, front delts, triceps

Cues

  1. Shoulder blades pulled back and down into the bench. Set this before you unrack and keep it. It is the difference between a chest exercise and a shoulder problem.
  2. Elbows roughly 45 to 75 degrees from your torso, not flared straight out to the sides.
  3. Bar touches around the lower chest and travels in a slight arc back over the shoulders on the way up.

Common mistakes

  • Fully flared elbows with the bar high on the chest, which is the classic shoulder impingement setup.
  • Bouncing the bar off the ribcage.
  • Benching alone in a rack with no safeties set.

Dumbbells are the better default if you train alone. You can drop them safely, each arm works independently, and the range is longer. Barbell wins on loadability.

Incline dumbbell press Upper chest, front delts

Cues

  1. Bench at 30 to 45 degrees. Steeper than that and it becomes a shoulder press.
  2. Lower until you feel a stretch across the chest. Dumbbells let you go deeper than a bar, so use that.
  3. Press up and slightly in, keeping a hand's width apart at the top.

Common mistakes

  • Short reps, stopping a third of the way down.
  • Letting elbows drift behind the torso under heavy weight.
  • Kicking the weights up from a bad start. Set up on your thighs, then lie back with them.
Overhead press Shoulders, triceps

Cues

  1. Brace the core and squeeze the glutes. Without this the lower back becomes the weak link.
  2. Move your head back slightly as the bar passes so it finishes over the mid-foot rather than out in front.
  3. Finish with the biceps by your ears. Full overhead, not a partial.

Common mistakes

  • Leaning back into a standing incline press.
  • Pressing the bar forward around your face instead of up past it.
  • Forcing a barbell overhead if your shoulders lack the range. Use dumbbells, where the path can vary.
Dip Chest, triceps

Cues

  1. Lean forward for chest, stay upright for triceps. Choose and stay consistent.
  2. Lower to about upper-arm-parallel, enough for a stretch without letting the shoulder take over.
  3. Shoulders down, away from the ears, throughout.

Common mistakes

  • Dropping into the bottom and bouncing out, the most common way people hurt a shoulder here.
  • Going very deep because someone on the internet said so.

If your shoulders complain, close-grip bench or a machine press does the same job. Nothing here is compulsory.

Pull pattern

Back and biceps. Most desk-bound people need more of this than they are doing, and it is the pattern most worth over-indexing on.

Chest-supported row Mid-back, lats, biceps

Cues

  1. Pull the elbows back and down toward your hips, not out to the sides.
  2. Pause for a second at the top with the shoulder blades squeezed together.
  3. Let the arms fully straighten at the bottom and allow the shoulder blades to travel forward. That is the stretch.

Common mistakes

  • Heaving with the lower back and hips.
  • Turning it into a biceps curl with bent-arm-only motion.
  • No pause and no control, just weight moving.

The best rowing variation for most people. The chest support removes the lower back, so the fatigue lands where you want it.

Pull-up and chin-up Lats, biceps

Cues

  1. Start from a full hang, then pull your shoulder blades down before the elbows bend.
  2. Drive the elbows toward the floor, chest up toward the bar.
  3. Chin over the bar, then lower all the way down under control. Full range every rep.

Common mistakes

  • Kipping and swinging to hide a lack of strength.
  • Half reps from the top down.
  • Never adding load once you can do 12. Hang a dumbbell from a belt or use a weighted vest.

Cannot do one yet? Use band-assisted or machine-assisted versions plus slow negatives: jump to the top and take five seconds to lower. Chin-ups with palms facing you are easier and hit biceps harder.

Lat pulldown Lats, biceps

Cues

  1. Grip slightly wider than shoulders, lean back about 15 degrees and hold that angle.
  2. Pull the bar to the collarbone, elbows driving down.
  3. Let the weight stretch you at the top, shoulders rising, then reset for the next rep.

Common mistakes

  • Rocking back and forth to move heavier weight.
  • Behind-the-neck pulldowns, which add shoulder strain for no benefit.
  • Very wide grip with a short range.
Single-arm dumbbell row Lats, mid-back

Cues

  1. Brace a hand or knee on the bench so your torso stays roughly parallel to the floor.
  2. Pull the dumbbell toward your hip, letting the shoulder blade move with it.
  3. Lower all the way until the arm is straight and the lat is stretched.

Common mistakes

  • Rotating the torso to swing the weight up.
  • Yanking with the elbow only, with no shoulder blade movement.
Face pull Rear delts, upper back

Cues

  1. Rope at upper-chest to face height. Pull toward your face with hands finishing beside your ears.
  2. Rotate outward at the end, knuckles back, like a double biceps pose.
  3. Light weight, 15 to 20 reps. This is not a lift to load heavily.

Common mistakes

  • Going heavy and turning it into an upright row with the whole body.
  • Shrugging the shoulders up.

Cheap insurance for shoulders, and it trains a region that heavy pressing leaves behind. Two or three sets a week is enough.

Arms and shoulders

Compounds do most of the work. These fill the gaps, particularly side delts, which nothing else trains directly and which do more for how you look in a shirt than any other muscle.

Lateral raise Side delts

Cues

  1. Lead with the elbow, slight bend in the arm, stopping at shoulder height.
  2. Tiny forward lean, thumbs level or slightly down. Think of pouring a jug at the top.
  3. Go light and slow, 12 to 20 reps. Most people use double the weight they should.

Common mistakes

  • Swinging the weight up with hips and momentum.
  • Shrugging, which means traps have taken over.
  • Raising well above shoulder height.

The most under-rated exercise on this page. Cable or machine versions keep tension more evenly than dumbbells.

Biceps curl Biceps

Cues

  1. Elbows stay roughly at your sides. If they drift forward, the front delts are helping.
  2. Full extension at the bottom every rep. The stretched position is where most of the growth is.
  3. Control the lowering. Three seconds down is more useful than 5 kg more weight.

Common mistakes

  • Swinging the torso back to start each rep.
  • Stopping halfway down.

Incline dumbbell curls train the stretched position hardest. Hammer curls hit the brachialis and add arm thickness. Two or three sets twice a week is plenty.

Triceps pushdown and overhead extension Triceps

Cues

  1. Elbows pinned to your sides on pushdowns. Only the forearm moves.
  2. Include one overhead variation. The long head only gets a full stretch with the arm overhead, and pushdowns never provide that.
  3. Full lockout at the end, full stretch at the start.

Common mistakes

  • Leaning over the cable and pressing with the whole upper body.
  • Elbows flaring wide on pushdowns.
  • Only ever doing pushdowns.

Core and calves

Short sections on purpose. Abs get trained by heavy compounds more than people think, and visible abs are a nutrition outcome rather than a crunch outcome.

Hanging knee raise and cable crunch Abs

Cues

  1. Curl the pelvis toward the ribs. The spine should flex, which is the job of the abs. Lifting straight legs is mostly hip flexors.
  2. Exhale hard at the top and squeeze for a beat.
  3. Load it. Abs are muscles, so 8 to 15 hard reps with added weight beats 100 crunches.

Common mistakes

  • Swinging on the bar with momentum.
  • Endless unloaded reps, which means no progression.
  • Expecting ab work to reveal abs. That is a calorie question.
Plank and side plank Bracing

Cues

  1. Ribs down, glutes squeezed, straight line from ear to ankle. A hard 30 seconds beats a sloppy three minutes.
  2. Breathe normally while holding the brace. That is the skill that transfers to squats and deadlifts.

Common mistakes

  • Hips sagging or piked up.
  • Chasing duration past 60 seconds. Add load or pick a harder variation instead.
Calf raise Calves

Cues

  1. Full stretch at the bottom, pause a second there. This is the part everyone skips and the part that matters.
  2. Rise all the way onto the toes and pause at the top.
  3. Slow and controlled. No bouncing on the tendon.

Common mistakes

  • Fast partial bouncing reps with a huge stack.
  • Two sets a week, then blaming genetics.

Seated versions bias the soleus and standing the gastrocnemius. Both are worth doing.

How much does perfect form matter?

Short answer

Less than form-policing content suggests and more than ego lifters believe. What matters is consistent execution, positions that do not hurt you, and full range of motion. Small deviations from a textbook picture do not.

  • Consistency of execution, so that adding weight means you got stronger rather than that you found a new way to cheat.
  • Positions that do not hurt you. A rounded lower back under a heavy deadlift, or a fully flared shoulder under a heavy bench, is where the real risk sits.
  • Range of motion, the most common thing separating people who grow from people who do not.

What matters less: small deviations from a textbook picture, whether your stance matches someone else's, and whether you look like a competitive lifter. Anatomy varies. The form that is strong and pain-free for you is the correct form.

How to check your own lifts: film one set from the side on your phone, once a month. You will spot depth, bar path and lockout issues immediately, and it is more useful than any amount of reading.

If something hurts

Stop the exercise and substitute a pain-free alternative in the same pattern. There is always one. Pain that persists beyond a few days, or any sharp, radiating or numb sensation, is a physiotherapist's job. Working through joint pain is how small problems become long layoffs.

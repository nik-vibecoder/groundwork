The nutrition pyramid

Three tiers. The base decides whether the diet works at all. The middle decides how healthy and liveable it is. The top is where the internet spends most of its attention for almost none of the result.

Short answer

Calorie balance sets your weight. Protein at 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of bodyweight protects your muscle. Consistency beats precision. Those three carry roughly 80% of the outcome.

Macro splits, fibre and food quality are worth 15 to 20% once the base is stable. Supplements and meal timing are worth 1 to 2%, and only creatine and caffeine have strong evidence behind them.

Tier 1

Calories and the deficit

Short answer

Set calories at 10 to 15% below maintenance to lose fat, at maintenance to recomp, or 5 to 15% above to gain muscle. Then adjust based on four weeks of scale data rather than trusting the formula.

Eat less energy than you burn and you lose weight. Eat more and you gain. Every diet that has ever worked did so by producing a deficit, not because of its rules. The rules are packaging.

You do not need to count forever. You need to count long enough to learn what your normal day actually adds up to, because most people are wrong by 20 to 40% in one direction. Two weeks of honest logging usually recalibrates you for life.

The formula is a first guess, not a truth

Any calculator, including ours, can be off by 15% for you personally.1 Use the number for two weeks, weigh yourself daily, compare weekly averages, then adjust by 150 to 200 kcal based on what actually happened. The scale is the instrument. The formula is only the starting point.

Sleep and steps belong here too

Neither is technically nutrition, but both sit in the foundation. Short sleep raises appetite, worsens food decisions, and shifts weight loss away from fat and towards muscle even when calories are controlled.20 Walking is the cheapest lever on total daily expenditure and the one that survives a desk job.18

Tier 1

Protein

Short answer

Eat 1.6 to 2.2 g of protein per kg of bodyweight per day, which is about 0.7 to 1.0 g per pound. Benefits for muscle gain plateau at roughly 1.6 g per kg, so the upper end is insurance and appetite control rather than extra growth.3

For an 80 kg person that is roughly 130 to 175 g a day. Sit higher in the range when you are cutting, since protein protects muscle when energy is scarce and keeps you fuller than the same calories from anything else.5

This is the one macro worth real effort, because it is the one most people miss by a wide margin. Hit protein and hit calories and the rest of the diet has a lot of freedom.

Tier 1

Consistency

A diet followed 85% of the time for a year beats a perfect one abandoned in week three. So build for the bad weeks rather than the good ones.

  • Assume travel, dinners and deadlines. A plan that only survives a calm week is not a plan.
  • Leave 10 to 20% of calories unassigned. Room for the thing you actually want, spent deliberately.
  • Repeat meals. Two breakfasts and three lunches on rotation removes most daily decisions.
  • Never start again on Monday. One heavy meal is one twenty-first of your week. Resume at the next meal.
Tier 2

Food quality and fibre

Short answer

Once protein is set, split the remaining calories between carbs and fat by preference. Keep fat above 0.6 to 1.0 g per kg. Aim for 25 to 38 g of fibre a day and get roughly 80% of your calories from whole, minimally processed foods.

Carbs and fat

Protein is set. Subtract it from your calories and divide what is left according to preference, respecting two floors:

Fat floor
0.6 to 1.0 g per kg of bodyweight, roughly 20 to 30% of calories
Carb floor
Enough to train hard, usually 3 g per kg and up if you lift 3 to 5 times a week

Above those floors the ratio has almost no independent effect on fat loss when calories and protein are matched. A 12 month randomised trial of 609 adults found no meaningful difference between low-fat and low-carb diets.6 Low-carb is not metabolically superior. It suits some people's appetite better. Choose the one you stop thinking about.

Fibre

Roughly 14 g per 1,000 kcal, so 25 to 38 g for most adults.7 It is the best single proxy for diet quality, because hitting it more or less forces fruit, vegetables, legumes and whole grains onto the plate. It also does more for hunger per calorie than anything else you can eat.

Whole foods, and why

Not because processed food is poison. Because it is engineered to be easy to over-eat and tends to be thin on fibre and micronutrients. Five hundred calories of chicken, rice and vegetables ends a meal. Five hundred calories of biscuits starts one.

Cover the boring stuff most days: a couple of fruits, two or three vegetable servings, a source of omega-3 such as oily fish twice a week, calcium, and enough variety that you are not relying on a multivitamin to patch the gaps.

Hydration and alcohol

Drink to pale yellow urine. Most people land near 2 to 3 litres a day including everything they drink. Coffee counts, since caffeine's diuretic effect is trivial at habitual doses.

Alcohol is not poison, but it is 7 kcal per gram, it blunts sleep quality and recovery, and it reliably loosens food decisions for the rest of the night. Budget it honestly as calories.

Tier 3

Supplements and timing

Short answer

Creatine monohydrate at 3 to 5 g a day and caffeine before training are the only two supplements with strong evidence for a healthy adult who eats enough protein.89 Protein powder is convenient food. Vitamin D, omega-3 and iron are worth taking only if a test or a restrictive diet says you are short. Everything else can be skipped.

Creatine monohydrate Strong evidence

Three to five grams daily, any time, with or without food. The most studied sports supplement there is: small but reliable gains in strength and training volume, well tolerated at these doses in healthy adults, and cheap.8

Monohydrate only. The fancier forms cost more and do no more. No loading phase is needed, since you saturate within three to four weeks either way. Expect 1 to 2 kg of early water weight, which is intramuscular and normal.

Caffeine Strong evidence, one catch

Three to six mg per kg, taken 30 to 60 minutes before training, improves performance and lowers perceived effort.9

The catch is sleep. Caffeine has a half-life of about five hours, and sleep is a tier one item. An evening pre-workout that costs you an hour of sleep is a net loss.

Protein powder It is food, not a supplement

Convenient, cheap protein. Whey, casein or soy, chosen on taste and tolerance. It has no special properties. If you hit your protein target with food you do not need it. If you do not, it is the easiest fix on this page.4

Vitamin D, omega-3, iron, B12 Test, do not guess

Worth taking if you are actually deficient or your diet genuinely lacks the source. Vitamin D if you are indoors in a low-sun climate. Omega-3 if you do not eat oily fish. Iron, B12 or calcium if a blood test or a restrictive diet indicates it.

Several of these have real upper limits, and iron in particular is harmful in excess. Get tested and speak to a doctor rather than guessing.

Everything else Skip

Fat burners, BCAAs (pointless if you eat enough total protein), testosterone boosters, detox teas, apple cider vinegar, greens powders used as a vegetable replacement, and most recovery blends. The supplement industry is large because supplements are easy to sell.

Nutrient timing

The anabolic window is hours wide, not minutes.10 If you have eaten protein in the few hours either side of training you are fine. Pre-workout carbs help if you train fasted and feel flat, which is a performance question rather than a physique one.

Common questions

Do carbs at night make you fat?

No. Total calories over the day and week decide that. Plenty of people eat most of their carbs at dinner and get leaner, because they are in a deficit. Meal timing has no meaningful independent effect on body composition.

Is sugar the problem?

Sugar is easy to over-eat and carries few nutrients, so most people benefit from less of it. It is not uniquely fattening at matched calories. The fruit in your yoghurt is not the issue. A 700 calorie dessert is, and only because of the 700.

Do I need to eat every three hours to boost my metabolism?

No. Meal frequency has a negligible effect on total energy expenditure. Eat on whatever schedule keeps you full and consistent. Three to five protein feedings of 25 to 40 g each is a practical structure, mainly because it is easier to hit a large daily total in four goes than in two.

Can I lose fat and build muscle at the same time?

Yes, if you are new to lifting, returning after a break, or carrying meaningful body fat. Eat at or slightly below maintenance, keep protein at the top of the range, and lift hard and progressively. The leaner and more trained you are, the harder this becomes.

Is a cheat day useful?

A single very large day can undo most of a week's deficit. A 2,000 calorie surplus is roughly four days of a modest deficit. A planned meal you enjoy without guilt works far better than a licensed binge.

Do detoxes or cleanses work?

No. Your liver and kidneys handle detoxification. Long periods of dieting do lower energy expenditure somewhat, through less spontaneous movement and less body mass, but the fix is a genuine break at maintenance rather than a juice.

Should I weigh myself every day?

Daily weighing combined with a weekly average is the most useful method, because it teaches you that a 1.5 kg overnight swing is water, salt and food volume rather than fat. If the scale genuinely damages your day, use weekly photos, waist measurements and how your clothes fit instead. The tool should reduce anxiety, not create it.
